## Session 1: Why the project model fails, and what the product model is

### The project model

Jon described a typical flow:

1. A stakeholder has an idea.
2. Analysts size the revenue.
3. An engineer is asked for a quick guess at how long it will take.
4. The idea lands on a roadmap with a date.
5. A PM or product owner gathers requirements.
6. A designer is pulled in to "make it pretty".
7. Engineers first hear about it at sprint planning.

He called this "fake agile", really waterfall.

### Why it fails

1. **Ideas come from the wrong place.** In the best companies, the best ideas often come from engineers, and here engineers aren't even in the room.
2. **Business cases give false precision.** The AWS business case put revenue somewhere between $1M and $1B.
3. **Roadmaps are just "prioritised lists of ideas" with dates.**
   - Even Google Search finds that 50–70% of its ideas don't work. For most teams it's closer to 80–90%.
   - The ideas that do show promise need 3–4 iterations before they deliver real value.
4. **Product management becomes a business-analyst role.**
5. **Design becomes "lipstick on a pig".**
6. **Engineers are mercenaries**, and you get about half their value.
7. **Agile alone comes too late.**
8. **Customer validation happens late.**
9. **Trust breaks down.** Leaders fire the team when the model is the root cause.
10. **Teams are measured on output, not outcomes.**

### AI is making this worse in many companies

> "It's never been faster to build, which means it's never been easier to run 10 times faster in the wrong direction." (Hilary Gridley, CPO, Whoop)

Using AI to speed up the project model:

- ships more features nobody uses
- moves the cost from engineers to tokens
- can raise running costs
- spends cycles building faster that could have gone to discovery

### The product model

**What a product is:** a technology-powered experience or capability that brings value to customers and also works for the business. It can face customers, enable customers, or be a platform.

**What the model is:**

- a set of principles drawn from the best companies, not a process or methodology
- a model where you fund durable teams and outcomes, not projects

### Three dimensions to change

| Dimension | Area | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| **How you build** | Product delivery | Release continuously, at least every other week. Everything is instrumented, with monitoring, A/B testing and rollback. Tech debt is often the biggest blocker. |
| **How you solve problems** | Product discovery | Teams are given problems to solve, not features to build. |
| **How you decide which problems to solve** | Product strategy | A product vision plus a product strategy. Without one you get the "peanut butter strategy", spread so thin you can't taste it. |

### AI has two lenses

1. **Using AI to create products**
   - Every role changes.
   - Delivery moves to continuous calibration.
   - Prototyping has never been faster.
2. **Building AI-powered products**
   - These products are probabilistic, not deterministic, so you optimise for a distribution of outputs.
   - The tech lead becomes an AI engineer.
   - Products drift over time, which makes continuous discovery mandatory.

### Product culture principles

- **Learning over failure.** Fail fast and cheap in discovery, which is different from failing at operational work.
- **Principles over process.** First-line managers coach teams instead of handing them process diagrams.
- **Trust over control.** "Move authority to the information" (L. David Marquet). That means empowerment, not full autonomy, and teams have to earn trust through competence.
- **Outcomes over predictability.** When a date genuinely matters, use a **high-integrity commitment**: a date given only after enough discovery. These cost you learning and flexibility, so use them sparingly.

### From the Q&A

- **Sales wants dates:** give them a *validated* roadmap, and bring sales closer to how the team works.
- **Moving off feature roadmaps:** change one thing first. Add the intended outcome under each feature, then send the data readout after release.
- **Getting finance on board:** offer the CFO pilot teams whose outcomes are tracked. One CFO stood up and applauded at a pilot readout.
- **Two backlogs:** keep one for discovery and one for delivery. Only validated work goes into delivery.

## Session 2: Teams, roles and leaders

### Empowered product teams

Empowered product teams:

- are cross-functional and durable
- are given problems to solve
- are held accountable for outcomes
- act as "missionaries, not mercenaries"
- collaborate closely with stakeholders

Jon dislikes the term "product-led" because stakeholders can hear it as a takeover.

### Product manager

Accountable for **value and viability** and for the team's outcomes. Backlog management, the product owner part, is only about 5–10% of the job.

**What to look for:**

- **High agency** ("there's no cavalry coming")
- **Trusted judgement:** show your reasoning openly
- **Natural collaboration:** co-creation, not consensus
- **Strong product sense:** earned, not innate

**How to build product sense:**

- Spend real time with customers.
- Spend real time with engineers.
- Master the data: user analytics and sales data.
- Understand stakeholders and the constraints the business works under.
- Follow what's happening in the industry.

**Also:**

- It's usually easier to teach domain knowledge to a strong PM than the other way round.
- One PM, one team. Splitting a PM across teams kills discovery.

### Product designer

Responsible for **usability and the experience of value**.

- This is a discovery role.
- It covers interaction, visual, service and content design plus user research.
- Expect 15–30 prototype iterations a week.
- AI doesn't replace strong designers.

### Tech lead

Responsible for **feasibility and delivery overall**.

- Co-invents solutions.
- Is the expert on what the technology (especially AI) can do.
- Thinks beyond their own codebase.
- "Cares as much about what they build as how they build."

Jon also argued that teams focused only on delivery are the ones likely to be outsourced to agents.

### Supporting roles

- **Delivery managers** remove blockers and report to engineering, not act as spies.
- **Product marketing managers** own go-to-market.
- **User researchers and data analysts** don't scale, so their job is to enable teams to self-serve.

### Product leaders

Product leaders enable and empower teams. Their responsibilities:

- Hire, coach and develop people. First-line managers should spend about 80% of their time on this.
- Set the vision.
- Gather insights from across the business.
- Set a focused strategy.
- Assign problems to teams.
- Make sure teams have access to customers, data and stakeholders.

**The two traps are micromanaging and abdicating.** Leaders should be "micro-interested", and "only experts can lead experts" (Apple).

### Spotify case study

*This part is missing from the transcript, so it comes from the slides.*

- **Strategy:** leadership chose to focus on underserved "lean-back" listeners.
- **Discovery:** two ML engineers led the work that produced Discover Weekly.
- **Risks tested:** value, usability, feasibility and viability.
- **Rollout:** employees first, then an A/B test with 1M users, then full rollout.
- **Results:** over 1B tracks streamed in 10 weeks.

Jon's point: *this* is the Spotify model, not "tribes and guilds".

## Session 3: Coaching, strategic context and product vision

### Coaching

Coaching is the alternative to scaling through process. Jon quoted Jassy, Pichai and Cook, who all name coaching as a top trait of good managers.

**How it works:**

- **Assessment:** this is *not* a performance review. Combine it with a self-assessment. Big gaps between the two are a useful conversation.
- **Development plan:** start with a gap analysis, then work on weaknesses and use strengths.

**Coaching with AI:** few managers can actually coach, so SVPG now recommends models as a personal coach.

- They're available 24/7.
- They're only as good as the strategic context you give them.
- This sets up the Trainline AI coach homework.

### Strategic context

The principle is **"lead with context, not control"** (Leslie Kilgore, Netflix). The leadership chain runs:

```
Business mission & objectives
        ↓
  Product vision
        ↓
  Team topology
        ↓
 Product strategy
        ↓
 Team objectives
        ↓
  Product teams
```

Without strategic context, teams make decisions that are only locally optimal.

**Principles:**

1. **Focus comes first.**
   - One CEO showed Jon 53 priorities for 100 engineers, which they cut to 15.
   - Jony Ive's point: focus means saying no to ideas you love.
2. **Transparency:** stakeholders bring their top problems with data, not solutions.
3. **Insights over opinions.**
4. **Placing bets:** "Improving decision quality is about increasing our chances of good outcomes, not guaranteeing them" (Annie Duke).

### Product vision

The future you're trying to create, told from the customer's side, typically 3–5 years out.

**Trainline:**

- A customer journey map showed most of the pain came *during* travel (delays, missed connections). That was downstream of the ticket sales that brought in all the revenue.
- The vision aimed at mobile and data, and at moving from UK to international and from rail to travel.
- It got engineering on board, led analysts to call Trainline a tech company, and was used in the IPO roadshow.

**Kaiser Permanente:**

- A vision video that unified many separate entities.
- It led to "Get Care Now", which was in place just before the pandemic.

**What a good vision does:**

- Puts the customer first.
- Gives one North Star (common intent), not one vision per team.
- Makes the work meaningful.
- Uses industry trends.
- Informs architecture and team topology.
- Reflects values.
- Works as your best recruiting tool.

**Communicate it relentlessly.**

> "Be stubborn on vision, but flexible on details." (Jeff Bezos)

AI now makes vision videos cheap to produce.
