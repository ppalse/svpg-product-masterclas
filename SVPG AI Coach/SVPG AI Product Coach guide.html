AI Product Coach:
From Context to Tested Prototype

Product Masterclass · Practicum Edition

You are a product manager. It is your first day at Trainline, on the Real-Time team. You have not met your wider team or any stakeholders yet. Your manager has given you an AI coach and the files that hold the context you need. Your job is to get up to speed fast.

Your coach works the way a strong product coach does. It shortens the time it takes to understand the context, raises the questions you should be asking and challenges your thinking. You do the work and make every decision.

Setup · paste into your workspace instructions
HOW TO COACH You are an expert product coach for product managers working in the product operating model. For product management advice, prioritize SVPG (www.svpg.com/articles), Marty Cagan, Shreyas Doshi and Teresa Torres. Your role: - Help me build context quickly and accurately. - Challenge my assumptions and reasoning. Do not just confirm them. - In the practicum, interrogate prototypes and risks more than you generate ideas. - Raise the questions I should be asking but haven't yet. - Ground every answer in the files you have, and cite numbers where they exist. Say when the documents do not answer a question. How we work together: - Keep answers under 250 words unless I ask for more. - If I refer to earlier work you can't see in this conversation, ask me to paste it. Never reconstruct it. - If my prompt still has a placeholder in [square brackets] that I should have filled in, ask me for it before you answer. - Value risk is the most often skipped. Weight it most. - Templates contain instruction notes. Follow them unless my prompt says otherwise. Where a note mentions a "session folder" or a file name you don't have, use the files in this workspace. - Before my first prompt, reply only to confirm in two sentences that you can read the files, then wait. Don't ask me questions or start coaching until then. I am responsible for all decisions. You are here to sharpen my thinking, not replace it. THIS EXERCISE I am a product manager at Trainline, Europe's leading digital rail and coach ticketing platform. I work on the Real-Time team, a mission team responsible for helping customers when their journey is disrupted. The team's goal is to move Trainline from a booking platform that abandons customers mid-journey to a travel companion that is present and useful when things go wrong. You have files covering the business overview, product vision, product strategy, key stakeholders, team topology, team OKRs, customer research, disruption analytics, brand guidelines and a set of product management templates. I am attending a three-day product masterclass: - Before the workshop: I build product sense on Trainline's business and customers with you. - Evening of Day 2: I build strategic context with you. - Day 3, the practicum: my team takes one problem. Each person builds a quick prototype alone, then plans alone how to test it across the four risks (value, usability, feasibility, viability). The team then compares the prototypes, chooses one, combines the test plans, tests what it can, iterates if there is time, and presents a readout.

What the setup prompt does

It gives the coach three things for the whole workshop: the scenario, its coaching role and the instruction to challenge you. Without it, the coach gives generic product advice.

3. Test your prototyping tool

On Day 3 of the masterclass, you build a prototype on your own in about 15 minutes. Make sure your tool works now. Use any tool that turns a written spec into a clickable prototype: for example Claude artifacts, ChatGPT canvas, Gemini Canvas, Lovable, v0, Bolt or Replit, or a tool your company has approved.

Paste the sample spec below into your tool and build it. If you get a clickable prototype in under 10 minutes, you are ready.

SampleSample spec: Expense Receipt

Paste everything below into your prototyping tool.

Objective: Let a business traveller send the receipt for a past trip to their finance team in under a minute, without searching their email.

Primary user: A sales manager who travels by train twice a week and files expenses every Friday.

Context: Receipts sit in booking confirmation emails. Every Friday the traveller searches their inbox, forwards each one and types the cost centre by hand.

Key tasks:

  • Find a past trip and open its receipt.
  • Add a cost centre and a short note.
  • Send the receipt to finance, or download it as a PDF.

Screens (mobile, three at most):

  • Screen 1, Your trips: three past trips as cards. "Tue 3 Nov · Manchester Piccadilly to London Euston · £86.40", button "Get receipt". "Thu 29 Oct · London King's Cross to Leeds · £72.10", button "Get receipt". "Mon 26 Oct · Bristol Temple Meads to London Paddington · £64.50", with a tick and "Receipt sent".
  • Screen 2, Receipt: booking reference TL48213, traveller name, route, date, "Anytime Return", fare £84.90, booking fee £1.50, total £86.40, "Paid by company card". A "Cost centre" field (placeholder "e.g. SALES-NORTH") and a "Note" field (placeholder "Client meeting"). Buttons: "Send to finance" and "Download PDF".
  • Screen 3, Sent: a tick and "Receipt sent to finance@yourcompany.com". Copy: "We've marked this trip as sent, so you won't send it twice." Button: "Back to your trips".

Brand: Trainline Mint #00A88F for actions, Deep Navy #160078 for headers, Success Green #00875A for sent. Status is always text plus colour, never colour alone.

Fake it: No real data, login or email. "Send to finance" moves to Screen 3. "Download PDF" can show a message instead of a file.

A1Before the workshop

Business Context

Your workspace holds the business context you need for your first planning meeting. Use it to learn what this company is and how it operates.

Prompt · paste into your chat
I've just joined as a product manager. It's my first day. Using the business documents you have, give me the briefing I need before my first planning meeting: 1. What this company is and how it makes money 2. The company's strategic direction over the next 12-18 months 3. The two or three most important tensions or constraints the business is facing right now 4. What I most need to understand before I walk into a room with my stakeholders Be direct. Not a summary of every document, just the things that will change how I think about product decisions here.

Context changes how you make decisions

Business context tells you which risks to take seriously, which stakeholders to involve early and which assumptions to test. An empowered PM gets up to speed fast and doesn't wait to be briefed.

A2Before the workshop

Meet the Customers

You have transcripts from 12 customers across three segments: business travellers, international tourists and families. They describe their journeys, what frustrated them and how they coped. Ask your coach for the patterns.

Prompt · paste into your chat
Read the customer research and tell me: 1. What are the three most important unmet needs across the three segments? Rank them, and say which segment feels each one most. 2. For each need, quote one verbatim line from a transcript, with the participant's name and segment. 3. What does this pattern mean for what my team should focus on? Rank by how many of the 12 interviews raise it and how severely, and name the interviews for each need. Quote exactly from the transcripts, not the research notes. If you can't find an exact line, say so rather than paraphrase. Keep it under 350 words.

Know your customers first-hand

PMs who decide well know the people they build for personally. That knowledge comes from regular, direct contact: sitting with customers, watching them struggle and hearing what they say in their own words. The research in this case is a start. At work, meet your customers yourself.

A3Before the workshop

Check the Quant Data

The transcripts gave you the qualitative picture. Now check it against the numbers. Interviews tell you what people feel. Data tells you how many are impacted, how often and how severe the issue is. You need both before drawing firm conclusions.

Prompt · paste into your chat
Using the analytics data alongside what we've established from the customer research, tell me: 1. Where do the numbers confirm what the transcripts said? 2. Where do the numbers contradict or complicate the qualitative picture? 3. What does the data reveal that the transcripts couldn't: scale, frequency, severity? Don't just summarize the data. Tell me what changes about our synthesis now that I have both signals.
A4Before the workshop

Coach Checks Your Context

You've quickly got up to speed on the business context, stakeholder landscape, and customer evidence. Before you move into strategy and objectives, tell your coach what you think matters most, in your own words, without looking back at the documents. The coach will take it from there.

Prompt · paste into your chat
Just finished reading through the Trainline context. Here's what I think matters most for my team right now, in four bullets, in my own words: [the customer problem that matters most] [the number that proves it] [the business constraint] [one thing I'm unsure of] If any line is still in brackets, ask me for it before you mark anything. Then mark each point holds / partly / wrong, citing the file, and tell me what I'm missing.
Fill in the highlighted parts before you send.
ExampleCheck your answer: what strong answers include

On the business model: The structural threat is a government-backed platform that would commoditize UK ticket sales and remove Trainline's booking fee entirely. Any product decision that touches pricing or UK rail has to take this threat into account.

On the customer problem: Customers trust Trainline to book but feel abandoned mid-journey. The largest NPS gap in the data is between customers who received any warning about a missed connection and those who received none. That gap is the team's primary lever.

On the team's role: The Real-Time team's success or failure directly tests the company's core strategic bet: that solving journey anxiety drives retention. It is the foundation of Trainline's case to investors and operators.

Part B · evening of Day 2

Strategic Context

B1Evening of Day 2

Product Vision

In Part B, your coach explains the product vision, product strategy, team topology and OKRs, then asks you a question to check your understanding. The vision comes first. It describes the future Trainline is trying to create.

Prompt · paste into your chat
I'm new at Trainline. Help me understand the product vision. 1. Explain it in under 150 words: the future Trainline wants by 2031, the problem it says it solves, and what it asks of product teams. 2. Then ask me one question: in one sentence of my own, what is Trainline's product vision? Wait for my answer. 3. When I answer, say plainly whether my answer is right, partly right or wrong, and what I missed, even if it's short. Then show me a strong answer and say which part of the document supports it. Then stop: don't ask anything else.
ExampleA one-sentence vision

Weak: "Trainline wants to be the best app for booking trains."

Strong: "By 2031 nobody in Europe should choose a flight because rail felt too risky, so Trainline has to stay with travellers for the whole journey, not just the booking."

Watch for: a sentence about booking or features. The vision is about removing the risk that makes people fly.

A vision gives direction

A product vision describes the future a company wants to create for its customers. It is not a feature list, a target metric or a mission statement. It gives every team the same direction, so their separate decisions add up. A PM who understands the vision can judge any piece of work against it.

B2Evening of Day 2

Product Strategy

The product strategy describes how Trainline plans to reach the vision: the few problems it has chosen to focus on, in what order, and what it has decided not to do yet.

Prompt · paste into your chat
Help me understand the product strategy. 1. Explain it in under 200 words: the bets Trainline is making, what it is choosing not to do yet, and where the Real-Time team fits. Use numbers from the strategy where it has them. 2. Then ask me one question: which two customer problems should the Real-Time team focus on this quarter, and why? Wait for my answer. 3. When I answer, say plainly whether my answer is right, partly right or wrong, and what I missed, even if it's short. Then show me a strong answer and say which part of the document supports it. Then stop: don't ask anything else.
ExampleTwo priorities

Weak: "Build delay notifications and launch delay prediction." These are features, not problems.

Strong: "1. Customers are left alone when a journey goes wrong, at the moment they most need help. 2. That has to work for international journeys too, because the strategy ties international growth to solving cross-border disruption."

Watch for: priorities that name things to build. Strong answers name customer problems and the gaps the strategy scores.

Context lets empowered teams decide

Empowered teams are trusted to make decisions. That trust only holds if they have the context to decide well. The vision describes the future the company wants to create, and the strategy describes how. The chain from vision to strategy to objective ties your work to the company's direction.

B3Evening of Day 2

Understand the Topology

The topology shows what each team owns, and which teams you depend on to get anything shipped.

Prompt · paste into your chat
Help me understand the team topology. 1. Explain it in under 200 words: the three kinds of team, the platform teams the Real-Time team depends on most and why, the channel teams we must work with, and the mission team whose work overlaps most with ours. 2. Then ask me one question: which dependency would most limit what we can promise customers on travel day? Wait for my answer. 3. When I answer, say plainly whether my answer is right, partly right or wrong, and what I missed, even if it's short. Then show me a strong answer and say which part of the document supports it. Then stop: don't ask anything else.
ExampleThe biggest dependency

Weak: "We depend on the App team."

Strong: "Supply and Journeys. Supply's feed quality decides what any live alert can promise, and Journeys owns whether a connection is at risk. I'd talk to both before promising a missed-connection alert."

Know who you depend on

Few teams can ship alone. Platform teams often decide what is technically possible. Customer-facing teams decide how your work reaches customers. Find these dependencies in discovery, before you promise anything.

B4Evening of Day 2

Meet Your Team's OKRs

The Real-Time team has two objectives and four key results (Team OKRs in Trainline Context Pack.pdf, page 79, under 'Real-Time (that's you)').

A good key result measures an outcome: a change for customers, such as more customers sorting out a problem on their own. A weak one measures output: something the team ships. Output can hit its target while customers are no better off.

Three of the four Real-Time key results measure outcomes. Your coach explains them all, then asks you to find the one that measures output.

Prompt · paste into your chat
Help me understand the Real-Time team's OKRs. Use only the section headed "Real-Time (that's you)" in Team OKRs, not other teams' OKRs. 1. In under 200 words, explain the two objectives and four key results (Objective 1 KR 1 and KR 2, Objective 2 KR 1 and KR 2): baselines, targets, what each measures, and where they pull against the strategy. Don't say yet which measure outcomes and which measures output. 2. Exactly one of the four measures output: it counts something the team delivers, not a change in what customers do or feel. Ask me which one and why, then wait for my answer. 3. When I answer, first test each of the four key results in one line: does it count something the team delivers, or a change in what customers do or feel? Only then say plainly whether I picked the right one. If I'm wrong, name the right one and say why. Don't repeat the question. Then carry on to step 4 in the same reply. 4. Check the output key result against the self-check in the Key-Results template: pass or fail per item, and say where the documents don't tell you. Then rewrite it as a customer outcome with a baseline from the analytics that measures exactly that. Say if no number fits, or if another Real-Time key result already covers it.
ExampleCheck your answer

Three key results measure outcomes, changes for customers: disrupted customers who found the information they needed (23% → 38%), 90-day churn after a missed connection with no warning (14.7% → 9%), and NPS from disrupted customers who used Trainline (+16 → +26).

The fourth measures output: the share of connection journeys where customers get proactive information (0% → 45%). It counts alerts sent, not what customers do with them. The team could hit 45% while customers still miss connections and still leave.

It is still a sensible key result for a capability that doesn't exist yet. Read it alongside the other three, which show whether customers are better off.

C2

Build Your Prototype

Day 3 Session 1 breakout · alone · 20 minutes

OutputA prototype spec, and a prototype built from it.

You do not have long, so use your time carefully.

Carry on in the chat you used for Parts A and B. If it has become long, feel free to start a new chat in your workspace instead. No workspace? Start a new chat, upload both PDFs and paste the setup prompt first.

Working alone first gives your team several different ideas to compare. A rough prototype is fine, as long as it makes one idea concrete enough to test.

C2a. Tell the coach your idea

Prompt · paste into your chat
Our team has chosen this problem from the Trainline list: [paste the problem] Ask me "What's your idea?" and wait for my answer. Don't suggest one. When I answer, reply only "Got it" and wait for my next prompt.
Fill in the highlighted parts before you send.

Answer in one or two sentences. Then send the next prompt.

C2b. Get your spec

Prompt · paste into your chat
Write a prototype spec for my idea that I can paste into a prototyping tool. I have about 15 minutes, so keep it small: one primary user, three key tasks, no more than three mobile screens, real UI copy, the Trainline brand colours from the brand guidelines, and a "Fake it" section listing what the prototype only pretends to do. The spec can be longer than your usual limit. Start the spec with a "Sample data" list: the customer's name, stations, train times and prices. Use only those values on every screen, and make sure the times add up and the stations are real for that route. Put the whole spec in one block headed "Paste this into your prototyping tool", in plain text with each item on its own line, not code or JSON, with no citations or source markers in it. Reply with only that block, then a three-line summary for my team: what it does, the key screen, and what customers must do for it to work.

Copy the block headed "Paste this into your prototyping tool" into your tool and build. If your prototyping tool is also your coach's tool, open a new chat outside your workspace and build there. If your tool doesn't work, sketch the three screens on paper instead. A sketch is still a prototype. Keep the three-line summary: you share it with your team later.

A prototype tests an assumption

A prototype that looks real can be mistaken for a decision. Yours exists to test one assumption quickly and cheaply. Expect most ideas to fail when customers try them. Discovery exists to find that out before you build.

C3

Plan Your Tests

Day 3 Session 2 breakout · alone · 15 minutes

OutputA short test plan for your own prototype: the riskiest assumption under each of the four risks, and one quick test for each.

Carry on in your chat. You share this plan with your team in the practicum.

Every product idea carries four risks. Name the riskiest assumption under each and plan a quick test, starting with value.

Prompt · paste into your chat
Help me plan how to test my prototype. Its spec is earlier in this chat. Go through the four risks, value first: - Value: will customers choose this over what they do today? - Usability: can a stressed customer work out how to use it at the moment they need it? - Feasibility: can we build it with the data and the platform teams we depend on? - Viability: does it work for the business: legal, commercial, operators, data and privacy? For each, give me one line: the riskiest assumption, its rating (Low, Medium, High or Blocker) with the evidence from the files or "no evidence", and one test. For value and usability, name a test a teammate could run with me in 5 minutes. For feasibility and viability, name the test I would run next week, and with whom. Today I can only test with teammates, so treat what they say as a hypothesis, not proof. For value, name a test with a real customer for next week where they have to decide something, such as signing up. Do not ask me for missing inputs. Name any gaps and carry on. Keep it under 150 words so I can share it with my team.
ExampleWeak and strong value assumptions

Weak: "Customers will like getting alerts."

Strong: "International tourists who get a connection-risk alert before they reach the station will act on it and save a Plan B train, instead of checking National Rail first." Specific about who, when and what they do, so you can test it.

C4

Choose One Prototype

Day 3 Session 3 practicum · as a team · 60 minutes for C4, C5 and C6

Suggested split: C4 show and choose 15 minutes, C5 plan 5, C6 test 15, record 5 and prepare the readout 15.

  1. Each person shows their prototype in one minute. Have it ready to share.
  2. Vote for the one most worth testing with customers. You can vote for your own.
  3. The winning prototype's owner is the driver. If it's yours, share your screen and run C5 and C6 in your chat.
  4. Everyone else sends the driver their summary from C2 and their test plan from C3.

After the vote, agree one sentence on why your choice is the best bet for customers.

C5

Combine the Test Plan

Day 3 Session 3 practicum, continued · as a team

OutputOne test plan for the chosen prototype, covering all four risks, including what you cannot test today.
Prompt · paste into your chat
We chose my prototype because [reason]. Here are my teammates' summaries and test plans: [paste what your teammates sent you] Combine my C3 plan, earlier in this chat, with the best tests from theirs into one test plan for my prototype. Give one short line per risk, in this order: value, usability, feasibility, viability. In each line: the assumption, the test, and the result that means proceed, iterate or stop. Test value and usability today, 5 minutes each, and say who plays which part. Feasibility and viability wait for next week: say who we'd test them with. Nobody here is a real customer. For value, a teammate who didn't build the prototype plays a customer from the research and has to make a real choice, such as signing up. Judge value by that choice, not by a score, and mark the result "hypothesis". Add a real-customer test for next week. For usability, a teammate who didn't build the prototype tries it. Don't ask me for missing inputs. Name any gaps and carry on. Keep it under 250 words.
Fill in the highlighted parts before you send.

Watch what people do, not what they say

Customers who nod are usually being polite. Value risk only comes down when a real customer acts: they sign up, pay, switch on notifications or refer someone.

C6

Test and Present

Day 3 Session 3 practicum, continued · as a team, then the main room

OutputTest results and a 5-minute readout.

Test, record what you saw, then prepare the readout. Leave time for the readout.

Run two tests. For value, a teammate plays a customer from the research. For usability, a teammate who didn't build the prototype tries it. Write down what you saw as it happens.

C6a. Record your results

Prompt · paste into your chat
Here is what happened in our tests: [for each test: what we did, who with, and what we saw] For each risk, judge the result against our test plan and decide whether to proceed, iterate or stop. Mark role-played results "hypothesis". Write "untested" only where no test was run. Add "value untested with real customers". For each result that says iterate, name one change to the prototype that answers it. Keep it under 150 words.
Fill in the highlighted parts before you send.

Time left? Iterate. Make the top change in your prototyping tool and run the usability test again.

C6b. Draft the readout

Prompt · paste into your chat
Draft our 5-minute readout for the VP Product in Key Stakeholders, as a speaker outline of about 250 words, three bullets per section at most: 1. Our problem, quoted in the customers' own words, the customer outcome we want and our biggest risk, with numbers from the files 2. The prototypes we considered, the one we chose, and why 3. What we learned about each risk and what we changed or would change. Say "value untested with real customers" 4. What we still need to test, and how 5. Our recommendation (proceed, iterate or stop) and the decision we need Cover the operator angle before we're asked. Use only what we found. Unless I say we made a change, describe it as one we would make.

Readouts and close

After the 60 minutes, everyone returns to the main room for 30 minutes. As many teams as time allows present, 5 minutes each, then questions. In the main room your instructor plays the product leader, and other attendees may give feedback. The day ends with a short close.
